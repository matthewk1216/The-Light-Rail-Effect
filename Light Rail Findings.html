Introduction

To establish a causal relationship, verifying the parallel trends assumption prior to an event study is essential. In the treated graphs to the right, a wide array of differing behaviors is immediately evident. However, in the specific case of station openings and the aforementioned anticipatory pricing, the parallel-trends assumption becomes trickier. This raises a more complicated question of the correct application of quasi-experiments to predict or model noisy, nuanced real-world phenomena. This point will be expanded upon in the conclusions section.

Census Tract - 1067.01

Immediately, an inversion is noticeable. The two tracts’ diverging and opposite behavior is worrisome if the parallel trends assumption must hold. Additionally, the treated tract’s downward sweep compared to the control tract’s upward may deem an anticipation false. This case may violate the parallel trends assumption and the anticipation hypothesis. It could be that the anticipation occurred before the data's time frame, but anticipation should, in theory, peak closest to the opening. Although if construction of the line proved to be a nuisance to proximal residents (who evidently have more elasticity in what they pay), and demand for housing in an area situated closely to an active construction site drops, then so too would prices. If this theory is correct, we should see a post-opening increase to make up for the earlier dip.

Census Tract - 4214

It's immediately apparent that more promising behavior exists than in the previous tract pair, though the two tracts don't display a perfect parallel. The general downward movement may indicate a common trend. It's also likely that the treated tract didn’t show anticipation. Both tracts share similar percentage changes of -1.1% and -1.0% in the year immediately before treatment, which could point to a direct parallel. Though for the parallel trend assumption to hold, this similarity should have been continuous throughout the period. Expecting mathematical equivalence between the points is unrealistic. No anticipatory effect likely exists in the treated, due to the perpetual decline. The conditions of both tracts are similar enough throughout to not completely rule out the parallel trends assumption.

Census Tract - 75.02

The movement evident arouses suspicion. While the treated tract shows consistent growth, the control tract’s dissimilar behavior invalidates a parallel trend. The treated tract’s 12.3% increase shortly before opening may point to early market anticipation. This anticipation could make it challenging for the null to conclude that an opening has a marginal effect. The station’s expected opening likely caused a rent change ahead of treatment, a crucial piece of information made more evident by further analysis of the pre-treatment timeline. This suggests that the simple lack of verification of a parallel trend is not disqualifying in this scenario.

Census Tract - 3101.01

The dip four years prior (-19.3%) is likely an anomaly, as the rent rebounds with a 32.4% increase, then stabilizes. The behavior directly following the rebound may confirm a stable pre-treatment market anticipation. At first, it seems both tracts follow a similar pattern. Still, upon closer inspection of the percentage changes, it is clear that they had dissimilar pre-treatment behavior, ruling out a parallel trend. Anticipation, while not definitive, is likely.

Census Tract - 57

There is a good chance that the percentage change increases two years prior to the opening in Census Tract 57, and the steady rise in the years before makes this an anticipatory effect. Comparing the specific percentage changes between the treated and control tracts makes this more apparent, as the control saw relatively minimal increases in the two years prior. A parallel trend can be firmly established as being non-existent; the two tracts hardly share any commonality. The lack of a parallel trend may cause issues for further analysis, giving way to a shaky foundation for any firm declaration of causal effects.

Census Tract - 4216.02

Averaging the movements of both tracts, the treated’s 1.4% increase contrasted with the controls' 6.3% and indicates drastically different behavior in a pre-opening period. The clear difference in average percentage change most likely points to a non-parallel trend. To avoid oversimplifying, the control tract has a median household income 62% higher than the treated's (as of 2024), and the treated has an 86.7% higher poverty rate. The control's steeper incline as a result of gentrification or revitalization, which the treated experienced less of, or not at all, could be a plausible explanation, but is speculative. The treated's modest, stable rise during this same window argues against a strong anticipatory effect, since rent would have been expected to lead rather than trail if anticipation were driving the trend.

Census Tract - 53.05

The treated and control tracts display similar growth in the beginning, but the treated diverges, increasing and widening the gap. The treated tract had an average percentage change of 8.3%; the control tract’s was 5.9%. This suggests a difference, which may threaten the parallel trends assumption. The treated tract's significant jumps shortly before a fall of 5.2% in the year before opening may indicate anticipation. While anticipation can't be completely established, it is fair to say the area saw growth. The transit may have been developed in an area of already rapid growth, rather than the transit being the source of that growth. This speculation can't be firmly established with the present data, though.

Census Tract - 3104

Both tracts in the three to five years before opening show broadly similar movement, which suggests a broader regional trend. The exact percentage points differ, but both tracts’ shared sharp rise in the year immediately before opening is still informative, since parallel trends graphs do not require identical movement but comparable behavior and direction. This commonality may be supported in the comparison of the average percent changes: the treated tract saw a 4.2% increase, in contrast to the control tract's 5.5%. These values do not differ immensely, but their closeness doesn't automatically equate to a parallel trend. Though mathematical equality between the two, when reviewing the data of something as disorderly as rent, isn’t realistic. The broad similarity between the two favors the parallel trends assumption, but it does not prove a firm conclusion.

Census Tract - 3102

The treated tract’s 2.3% average compared to the control tract’s 11% suggests a significant difference. The treated tract’s comparatively lower rise complicates the interpretation of an anticipatory effect. Census Tract 3102 has a $132,929 median household income, compared to Census Tract 1000.01’s $102,588. Census Tract 3102 has a 12.9% poverty rate; Census Tract 1000.01 has a poverty rate of 11.2%. The socioeconomic profiles share a similarity. Despite some socioeconomic similarity, the pre-treatment trends differ enough that the parallel-trends assumption is less convincing.

Census Tract - 2.02

In the year before opening, the tracts diverge: the control tract increases while the treated tract decreases. This divergence is also visible in the three years before opening, when the treated tract increases as the control tract decreases. In the four years prior, the control tract grows substantially more than the treated tract. These patterns of divergence suggest that the parallel trend assumption does not hold. If the values had increased together, this would have been more plausible. The treated tract’s dip in the year immediately before opening doesn't outright invalidate an anticipatory effect, but it does show that the pre-treatment path is not stable.

Methodology

Using an event study with this type of data poses issues because the treatment timing is staggered across station openings. A standard Two-Way Fixed Effect (TWFE) difference-in-differences model requires all groups to be treated at the same time, which is not the case here given the clear differences in opening dates between the tracts. As a result, using the TWFE model would produce biased results and fail to account for the heterogeneous nature of the treatments. To address this issue, the Sant'Anna and Callaway event study model was utilized because it accommodates datasets with differing treatment times, i.e., staggered treatment.
